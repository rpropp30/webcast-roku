1Electrical Theory

Goal: make the science usable~45 min · slides 5–24

Drive these home
  • Current, not voltage, injures and kills — voltage just pushes it through your resistance.
  • Heat and arc energy rise with the square of current (P = I²R).
  • We transmit high (low current, low loss) and use low — but low voltage still kills.
  • Transformers enable the grid; a secondary can be back-fed by a customer generator.
Discussion prompts
  • Trace the path of power from the plant to this building. Where are we on that chain?
  • Why does a shock get far more dangerous when you're sweaty or it's raining?
Common misconceptions to correct
  • "High voltage is dangerous, low voltage isn't." — Most contacts happen below 600 V.
  • "AC is off every time it crosses zero." — It's lethal continuously at 60 Hz.
Hands-on / demo
  • Work the Ohm's-law triangle live; show how lower R raises I for the same V. Sketch the local feeder one-line.

2Electricity & the Human Body

Goal: make the hazard personal~35 min · slides 25–40

Drive these home
  • The lethal range is in milliamps — far below what trips a breaker.
  • Path matters: hand-to-hand / hand-to-foot crosses the heart.
  • Arc flash/blast can injure without contact — distance and FR PPE are the defense.
  • Energized ground creates step & touch potential — shuffle out, feet together.
Discussion prompts
  • Right now, what would your second point of contact be if this rail were energized?
  • A car hits a pole and the line is down on it — what do you tell the driver, and why?
Common misconceptions
  • "It didn't knock me down, so it was harmless." — Burns and arrhythmia can be delayed.
  • "My boots/gloves make me untouchable." — Only when rated, inspected, and the second point is controlled.
Hands-on / demo
  • Walk the step-potential shuffle. If available, show an arc-flash incident-energy example for your gear.

3Working Around Energized Lines

Goal: the daily survival disciplines~60 min · slides 41–63 · CORE

Drive these home
  • Work the hierarchy of controls top-down — de-energize whenever feasible.
  • The second point of contact completes the circuit — find it, cover it, remove it, every time.
  • Cover the nearest hazard first; remove cover-up in reverse.
  • LOTO: identify → open → lock/tag → test → ground. Ground end first, line end last.
  • An equipotential zone defeats re-energization and induced voltage.
Discussion prompts
  • On this pole-top photo, name every possible second point of contact.
  • When is energized work actually justified instead of de-energizing?
Common misconceptions
  • "Rubber gloves alone are enough." — You must also control the second point (cover-up / position).
  • "Dead means safe." — Dead is not grounded; induction and reclosers bite back.
Hands-on / demo
  • Run the cover-up sequence on a mock pole; build an EPZ; measure MAD with a stick. Have apprentices narrate each step.

4Tools of the Trade

Goal: trust your gear — and prove it~40 min · slides 64–76

Drive these home
  • Rubber gloves go with leather protectors; pick a class at or above your voltage.
  • Test the tester on a known source — before and after.
  • Size grounds for the available fault current; ground end first.
  • PPE is the last line — it limits injury, it doesn't prevent contact.
Discussion prompts
  • What takes a tool or glove out of service immediately?
  • Where do the numbers printed on your gloves and grounds come from?
Common misconceptions
  • "They look fine, so they're fine." — Honor air-tests, inspections, and retest dates.
  • "Any glove for any job." — Class must match the voltage.
Hands-on / demo
  • Glove air-test and inspection; handle a hot stick; apply and remove a ground set in the correct order.

5Putting It to Work in the Field

Goal: turn knowledge into routine~45 min · slides 77–90

Drive these home
  • Plan the job — and the rescue — before boots leave the truck.
  • The tailboard covers hazards, procedures, precautions, energy controls, and PPE.
  • Use three-way communication; re-brief on any change.
  • Everyone has — and must use — stop-work authority.
Discussion prompts
  • Let's run a real tailboard for tomorrow's job — who takes which role?
  • If a worker contacts a conductor at the pole top, what happens in the next 60 seconds?
Common misconceptions
  • "The briefing is just paperwork." — It's where the plan becomes shared.
  • "We'll figure out rescue if it happens." — Rescue is planned and practiced.
Hands-on / demo
  • Full tailboard role-play with role cards; pole-top or bucket rescue drill with the handline and AED trainer.

6Regulations & Standards

Goal: know the why and the where~25 min · slides 91–100

Drive these home
  • Regulations set the minimum; your program may be stricter — follow the stricter.
  • OSHA 1910.269 / 1926 Subpart V govern the work; NESC governs the system.
  • Only qualified employees work on or near energized parts.
Discussion prompts
  • Which standard backs MAD? Cover-up? Glove ratings? Grounds?
  • Where is your employer's MAD table, and when was it last updated?
Common misconceptions
  • "Compliance equals safe." — Compliance is the floor, not the goal.
  • "Any trained worker can do it." — Qualification is specific and documented.
Hands-on / demo
  • Locate a specific rule in 1910.269; walk your employer's approved MAD table together.

7Arc-Flash Analysis, First Aid & Rescue

Goal: the numbers behind the PPE, and what to do when someone is hurt~45 min · Module 7

Drive these home
  • Incident energy (cal/cm²) rises with fault current & clearing time and falls with distance².
  • The arc-flash boundary is where energy = 1.2 cal/cm²; the PPE arc rating must meet or exceed the incident energy.
  • Read the label and set your boundaries before opening equipment.
  • Rescue order is fixed: make the scene safe → reach the patient → treat. Never become the second victim.
Discussion prompts
  • Pull up a real arc-flash label — what PPE and boundaries does it require?
  • A coworker is down in contact with a conductor — talk me through your first 60 seconds.
Common misconceptions
  • "Same voltage means same arc-flash hazard." — Fault current, clearing time, and distance dominate.
  • "Grab them off the line fast." — Not until the source is removed; speed never overrides scene safety.
Hands-on / demo
  • CPR & AED practice on a manikin with the AED trainer; run a downed-line scene-control drill (barrier, call, don't touch the patient).
This module supports, but does not replace, hands-on CPR/AED certification. Ensure crews hold current first-aid/CPR/AED credentials.

✎Assessment & Sign-Off

Knowledge
  • Assign the quiz — full set, or one module at a time after each segment. Review every missed explanation.
  • Target mastery, not just a passing percentage; re-take modules until solid.
Skill (hands-on competency check)
  • Identify second points of contact on a pole top.
  • Demonstrate cover-up: nearest first, remove in reverse.
  • Apply & remove a ground set in the correct order.
  • Air-test and inspect a rubber glove.
  • Lead a complete tailboard and state the rescue plan.
Document both the knowledge check and the hands-on demonstration on your competency sign-off sheet, with dates and the evaluator's name.

⚙Adapting the Course

  • New apprentices: deliver all six modules across multiple sessions; spend extra time on Modules 1 and 3 and repeat the hands-on demos.
  • Journeyman refresher: compress theory, focus on Module 3, recent incidents/near-misses, and the tailboard role-play.
  • Toolbox / short form: pick a single module's diagram slides and its quiz module for a 15-minute talk.
  • Localize every numeric table to your system, gear, and procedures before delivering.
